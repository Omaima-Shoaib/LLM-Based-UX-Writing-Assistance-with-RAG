This guide is for product designers, content designers, UX writers, engineers adding microcopy, and any team member who writes words that appear in the product — including error messages, tooltips, labels, push notifications, and onboarding flows.

UX writing is not just labeling buttons. In a fintech context, poor writing can erode user trust at the moment of payment, create confusion during security checks causing unnecessary drop-off, generate support tickets from unclear error messages, and expose the company to compliance risk if authentication language misrepresents what a process does. Good UX writing does the opposite: it guides users through complex flows with calm, clear language that makes them feel safe and in control.

Voice is who we are across every interaction. Tone is how we adjust to each situation. Voice stays consistent; tone adapts.

The standard  brand voices of tone are Clear, Trustworthy, Calm, Human, and Concise, Being Clear means using plain language with no jargon and no unexplained acronyms — write "Your payment is being verified", not "EMV 3DS challenge initiated". Being Trustworthy means being accurate, honest, and precise, never minimising risk or overclaiming security — write "Secured with Visa Secure", not "100% fraud-proof". Being Calm means avoiding alarming language especially during authentication steps — write "We need to verify it's you", not "Security alert: action required". Being Human means writing like a helpful person, not a legal document or a robot — write "Something went wrong. Let's try that again.", not "Error code 4031". Being Concise means respecting the user's time and saying only what they need to know right now — write "Confirm payment", not "Please proceed to confirm your payment transaction".

These general writing rules apply to all microcopy across the product, regardless of component or situation. Don't over-explain — communicate only essential details and include only the information that helps the user complete the specific task at hand, avoiding information overload especially during payment and authentication flows. Be concise, not robotic — write short, scannable text, since users in a payment flow are focused and should get only what they need. Write simply and directly, since plain language is not dumbing down but respecting the user's attention; always ask if there is a simpler way to say something. Start with the user's goal so sentences mirror how users think and speed up comprehension. Use common, everyday words that any reader would understand on first read, and avoid jargon and unexplained acronyms — technical protocol names like EMV, 3DS, VbV, PAN, or tokenisation belong in developer or support documentation only, never in consumer-facing UI unless explained. Write in the present tense rather than future tense. Use numerals (1, 2, 3) instead of spelled-out numbers in UI text and instructions. Refer to UI elements by their label, not their type — say "tap Pay" not "tap the Pay button". Use consistent terms across all screens for the same action — never use "confirm", "approve", and "authorise" interchangeably. Use verb-first, specific labels for buttons rather than generic ones like OK, Yes, No, Submit, or Click here. Avoid alarm words such as "alert", "warning", "danger", "threat", "breach", or "suspicious" in routine flows, since these cause unnecessary anxiety and can increase abandonment. Never overclaim security with phrases like "100% secure", "fraud-proof", or "guaranteed safe", since these are inaccurate and create legal exposure. Never show raw error codes to users — log them internally and show a human message instead. Address users in second person (you/your) as the default, using first person (I/my) only when the user is taking clear ownership of a choice or piece of content, and never mix the two perspectives in the same phrase or sentence.

During onboarding and first use, the tone should be warm and encouraging, with short sentences, positive framing, and a welcoming feel. During a payment in progress, the tone should be calm and reassuring, using present tense, factual language, and minimal words. During authentication or a Visa Secure step, the tone should be calm and authoritative — precise, free of alarm, and always explaining the reason. For success confirmations, the tone should be warm and brief — positive, closing the loop quickly, without over-celebration. For user-fixable errors, the tone should be supportive and instructive, explaining what happened and what to do next. For system errors, the tone should be apologetic and calm — own the problem, give next steps, and place no blame on the user. For security warnings, the tone should be serious and direct, using clear language, avoiding panic, and staying actionable. For empty states, the tone should be helpful and light, guiding the user toward action and avoiding dead-end phrases like "Nothing here".

When writing for the product, be concise and not robotic: write short, scannable text. Users in a payment flow are focused — give them only what they need. Write "Confirm payment" not "Please proceed to confirm your payment". Write "Add your card details" not "Please enter your card information below". Write "We couldn't process this" not "An error has occurred during payment processing".

Write simply and directly: plain language is not dumbing down — it is respecting the user's attention. Always ask: is there a simpler way to say this? Write "Verify your identity" not "Authenticate your credentials". Write "Your card has been added" not "Card tokenisation was successful". Write "Try again" not "Re-initiate the transaction".

When addressing users, use second person (you/your) as the default. It is more direct and feels human. Use first person (I/my) only when the user is taking clear ownership of a choice or piece of content. For general instructions, use "Your payment is ready" rather than "The payment is ready". For the user's own content in navigation, use "My cards" or "My account" rather than "Your cards". Never mix "my"/"me" and "you"/"your" in the same phrase or sentence. Mixing perspectives creates confusion about whose point of view the product is speaking from.

Communicate only essential details: every screen has a job. Only include information that helps the user complete that specific task. Avoid information overload, especially during payment and authentication flows. Write "Enter the 6-digit code we sent to +20 .... 4521" not a sentence explaining that a one-time verification code has been dispatched to your registered mobile number for security purposes. Write "3 attempts remaining" not "You have 3 remaining attempts before your account is temporarily suspended". Write "Card ending in 4242 charged 49.99" not a full sentence restating the card type, digits, and amount.

Start sentences with the user's goal: structure sentences so the user's objective comes first. This mirrors how users think and speeds up comprehension in checkout and payment flows. Write "To add a card, tap the + icon" not "Tap the + icon if you would like to add a card". Write "To pay, confirm your amount below" not "Confirm your amount below if you'd like to pay". Write "To cancel, tap Back" not "Back should be tapped in order to cancel".

For general word choice, use common, everyday words that any reader would understand on first read. Write in the present tense: "Your payment is processing" not "Your payment will be processed". Use numerals (1, 2, 3) not spelled-out numbers in UI text and instructions. Refer to UI elements by their label, not their type — say "tap Pay" not "tap the Pay button". Use consistent terms across all screens — never use "confirm", "approve", and "authorise" interchangeably for the same action.

For fintech-specific word choices, write "Verify your identity" not "Authenticate" or "authenticate credentials" — plain language is less technical. Write "Payment" not "Transaction" or "disbursement" — it is the most widely understood term. Write "Code" or "passcode" not "OTP" or "one-time password" — avoid acronyms without explanation. Write "Card details" not "Card credentials" or "PAN" — everyday language serves users better. Write "Something went wrong" not "Error", "system failure", or "exception" — it is human and non-alarming. Write "We couldn't complete this" not "Transaction declined" or "rejected" — it is less confrontational. Write "Secured with Visa Secure" not "3DS", "EMV 3DS", or "3-D Secure" — always use the brand name in consumer UI. Write "Learn more" not "Click here", "find out more", or "read more" — it is standard accessible link text. Write "Try again" not "Retry" or "re-initiate" — it is plain and direct. Write "Add card" not "Register card" or "add payment instrument" — it is concise and familiar.

Avoid these word-choice patterns: never expose technical protocol names such as EMV, 3DS, VbV, PAN, or tokenisation directly in consumer-facing UI unless they are explained. These terms belong in developer or support documentation only. Avoid words like "alert", "warning", "danger", "threat", "breach", or "suspicious" in routine authentication flows — they cause unnecessary anxiety and can increase abandonment rates. Never say "100% secure", "fraud-proof", or "guaranteed safe". These claims are inaccurate and create legal exposure. Use "secured with Visa Secure" or "verified by Visa" instead.

When the app displays the Visa Secure badge or references the Visa Secure program, the following writing and naming rules apply. These are derived from Visa Secure Brand Standards (September 2025).

For Visa Secure naming rules, always write the program name as "Visa Secure" — two words, both capitalised. Never abbreviate, translate, or alter either word. Correct usage is "Visa Secure" and "Secured with Visa Secure". Incorrect usage includes "visa secure", "VISA SECURE", "Visa-Secure", "3DS", "EMV 3DS", "Verified by Visa" in consumer UI, or "Secured by Visa's 3-D Secure system". The words "Visa" and "Secure" must not be translated into any other language. Supporting text such as "Learn more" may be translated to the local language equivalent.

For Visa Secure badge display rules, the badge may only be used once per page or screen. It must be equal in visual prominence to any other payment authentication branding shown. Use only Visa-provided artwork from visa.com/brand — do not recreate or alter the badge. Do not place a trademark symbol (TM or R) next to the badge. Do not use the badge to imply endorsement of a product, service, or merchant. Do not use the badge as an acceptance mark — it is an authentication mark, not a payment acceptance mark. Do not contain the badge within a shape such as a circle or square cutout.

For Visa Secure badge color, the preferred version is Visa Blue on a white or light background, used as the default for all app screens. The second option is Visa Blue on a dark or black background with a white keyline, used for dark mode or dark card backgrounds. The third option is black and white, used only when color is not supported. The last resort is Visa Secure in text only, used only when graphic display is impossible.

For authentication screen writing, when a Visa Secure authentication step is triggered, explain why the step is happening in plain language. Use calm, factual language — this is a routine security check, not an emergency. Reference Visa Secure by name when appropriate to reinforce trust. Keep screens concise with one primary message and one action. Write "We need to verify it's you" not "SECURITY ALERT: Identity verification required". Write "Your bank has requested a security check" not "EMV 3DS challenge initiated by issuer". Write "Secured with Visa Secure" not "Verified by Visa 3-D Secure". Write "Enter the code sent to +44 .... 7823" not "Input your OTP / one-time passcode". Write "Didn't receive it? Resend code" not "Re-initiate OTP delivery".

For "Learn more" link text related to Visa Secure, the Visa Secure badge may appear with a "Learn more" link. The link text must read "Learn more" or an approved equivalent — contact Visa if using alternative phrasing. "Learn more" may be translated into the local language. The link must lead to a Visa-provided Visa Secure service description page, or an issuer or merchant security information page that explains what Visa Secure is. The service description page must explain what Visa Secure is and how to get more information. The approved service description page template reads: "As a [Issuer name] Visa cardholder, your transaction is secured with Visa. To learn more, click the 'Learn more' link below."

For dialog writing, dialogs are highest priority. They block app usage and require an explicit user decision. Reserve them for actions with real consequences: irreversible payments, cancellations, or security actions. The title should be a short, clear statement or question that states what is happening or what the user must decide. The body should contain only the essential detail and any critical consequence. Buttons CTA should use action-first labels that make the choice obvious. Write "Cancel this payment?" not "Are you sure?" for the title. Write "49.99 to Netflix will not be charged. This cannot be undone." not a long passive sentence about the transaction being cancelled and reversed. Write "Cancel payment" on the buttons, not "Yes / OK / Confirm" and "No / Cancel / Go back".

For snackbar and toast writing, snackbars are low priority and disappear automatically. Use them for confirmations of routine actions. Keep them to 1–2 lines. State what just happened, using past tense for completed actions. Optionally include one action button if the user may want to undo or view details. For a payment sent, write "Payment of 49.99 sent" with an optional "View receipt" action. For a card added, write "Card added" with an optional "Manage cards" action. For a card removed, write "Card removed" with an optional "Undo" action. For saved settings, write "Changes saved" with no action needed.

For error message writing, errors are where trust is most at risk. A good error message has three parts: what happened, why if helpful, and what to do next. For a user input error, state what is wrong and what to fix — write "Card number must be 16 digits". For a declined payment, state the outcome calmly and suggest an action — write "We couldn't complete this payment. Try a different card or contact your bank." For a system or network error, own the problem and give next steps — write "Something went wrong on our end. Please try again in a moment." For a session timeout, explain why and offer a path forward — write "You've been signed out for your security. Sign in to continue." For a failed authentication, stay calm and offer a retry or help — write "We couldn't verify your identity. Try again or contact your bank."

Never show raw error codes such as "Error 4031" or "ERR_AUTH_FAIL" to users. Log them internally and show a human message instead. If a code is legally required, place it in small secondary text below the main message.

For primary button and label writing, use verb-first labels: "Pay now", "Add card", "Verify identity", "Send money". Be specific — "Confirm" is weaker than "Pay 49.99". Primary button CTA text should complete the sentence "I want to...". Avoid generic labels like OK, Yes, No, Submit, or Click here. Write "Pay 49.99" not "Submit". Write "Add card" not "Add payment method". Write "Verify identity" not "Authenticate". Write "Send to James" not "Send". Write "Try again" not "OK".

For secondary button writing, use clear, action-oriented labels that describe the alternative path available to the user. Secondary button CTA text should help users postpone, review, return, or exit an action without creating uncertainty. Be specific — "Review details" is clearer than "Back". Secondary button text should clearly communicate what happens next and should never force users to guess the outcome. Avoid generic labels such as Back, Cancel, No, Close, Dismiss, or OK when a more descriptive alternative is possible. Write "Review details" not "Back". Write "Maybe later" not "Cancel". Write "Return to card settings" not "Close". Write "Choose another card" not "No". Write "Continue editing" not "Dismiss". Secondary CTAs should reduce ambiguity, support user control, and provide a clear alternative to the primary action.

For empty state writing, empty states should guide users toward action, not just announce that nothing is there. For a screen with no cards added, write "Add a card to start paying" not "No cards". For a screen with no transactions, write "Your payments will appear here" not "No transactions yet". For a screen with no notifications, write "You're all caught up" not "Nothing here". For a screen with no search results, write "No results for '[query]'. Try different words." not "No results".

For push notification writing, notifications must be useful, timely, and clear. Users who find them confusing or alarming will disable them, losing a critical trust channel. Lead with what happened, then the detail. Use numerals and be specific about amounts and recipients. Avoid alarm words for routine events. For security events, be direct but calm. For a payment sent, write "Payment sent • 49.99 to Netflix". For a card charge, write "Charge confirmed • 12.50 at Starbucks". For a required authentication, write "Verify your payment • Tap to confirm 49.99 to Amazon". For unusual activity, write "Check your account • We noticed an unrecognised payment attempt". For a card expiring, write "Card expiring soon • Update your card by 31 Dec".

Accessible writing is not a separate consideration — it is part of good writing. These rules ensure the app works for users with visual impairments, screen readers, and cognitive differences.

For alt text on images and badges, every image must have descriptive alt text up to 125 characters. Do not start with "Image of" or "Picture of" — screen readers announce that automatically. For the Visa Secure badge, alt text should read "Visa Secure" — do not describe its visual appearance. For payment card illustrations, describe the card type and last 4 digits if visible, for example "Visa card ending in 4242".

For labels and controls accessibility, all interactive controls must have a visible, descriptive label. Button text must describe the action, not the state — write "Pay now" not "Active" or "Enabled". Form fields must have labels that remain visible when the field is filled — do not rely on placeholder text alone. Error messages must be programmatically associated with the input they refer to.

For reading level, aim for a reading level accessible to any adult user. Keep sentences short — under 20 words where possible. Use one idea per sentence. Choose common words over technical terms. Use active voice over passive voice.

The content matrix is used to track, audit, and maintain writing decisions across the product. Every piece of recurring UI text should have an entry. Each entry should record the screen or component where the text appears, the user's goal at that moment, the information the user needs to know in plain non-UI language, a draft version of the UI copy, the final approved live text, the tone applied, the component type such as dialog, snackbar, button, label, or notification, words to use in that context, words to avoid, whether Visa Secure rules were applied and which ones, whether accessibility was checked including alt text, label, and WCAG AA pass, and who is responsible for the content and when it was last reviewed.

Before any UI text goes live, run through this quick reference checklist. For writing quality, check: is the message under 20 words? Does it start with the user's goal? Is it in present tense? Does it use common words with no jargon and no acronyms? Does it say only what the user needs to know right now? Is the button label a verb that completes "I want to..."?

For voice and tone, check: does it match the tone for this moment? Is it using you/your or I/my for owned items — but not both? Is it calm with no alarm words and no overclaiming of security? Does it sound like something a helpful person would actually say?

For error messages, check: does it state what happened? Does it say what to do next? Does it avoid raw error codes in the user-facing text? Is it supportive and non-blaming?

For Visa Secure, if applicable, check: is the program name written as "Visa Secure" with both words capitalised? Is the badge using Visa-provided artwork with no alterations? Is the badge appearing only once per screen? Does the "Learn more" link lead to an approved Visa Secure description page? Are the words "Visa" and "Secure" not translated? Is the badge free of trademark symbols? Is it not being used as a payment acceptance mark?
